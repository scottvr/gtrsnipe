# Rhythm in gtrsnipe's tabs: the dash-count layout

*Agreed 2026-10-07; not built yet (backlog F08). The measurements quoted are from 300
Nottingham folk tunes written out as tabs.*

## A Brief History of Timing

Early in gtrsnipe's development, while wanting to ensure nice-looking tabs, while also wanting
to encode at least a bit of rhythmic timing information, I experimented with a few different ways
of codifying what, for me when hand-transcribing had always been a sort of gut intuition based
(with lots of trial-and-error revision when a double-digit fret entered the scene making things look wonky, or
colliding with a column on an adjacent string causing me to add a dash and adjust by one everything to the right of the newly added
two-digit fret indicator) "system". I wanted to codify something so that gtrsnipe's generated tabs were consistent,
and further, that if a .tab was used as a source to output another format (e.g., .mid) that *does* precisely encode rhythm,
the output would at least be sensible, while also not just being a metronome.

Recently, having added actual playback (audio and visual) to gtrsnipe, I had Claude run a bug-sweep of
the current codebase, and he found (funnily enough) an "off-by-one-column" bug with double-digit frets in the reading and writing of the
"rhythmish" spacing in gtrsnipe's state at the time of his audit. This bringing the topic to the fore, I explained to Claude my memory
of the early experiments with clandestinely encoding exact rhythm in traditional ascii tab without making them noticeably weird to
a human reader, while enabling gtrsnipe to more accurately render to formats that do contain precise rhythm alongside pitches. The goal
was to replace the note duration information informally suggested by tabs in-the-wild by varied spacing, and to have exact duration
information available when reading a tab previously generated by gtrsnipe.

I wasn't sure how many of my old experiments had ever actually been committed to the repo, nor when or why I eventually abandoned them,
but Claude was up to the task and searched git history, coming back with this information from his archaeological dig:

1. December 2024 (`fd583c0`, `src/gtrsnipe/core/tab.py`): strictly proportional, 12
   characters per beat, with a line of beat numbers over the staff as a ruler. Exact, and
   48 columns for every 4/4 bar.
2. July 2025 (`46ccf63` to `8979623`, `_get_quantized_spacing`): a step function with the
   shape of a logarithm. A note one unit long got 2 dashes, two units 3, four units 4,
   longer 5, and the parser carried the inverse (`_spacing_to_beats`). So it was a code:
   the *number* of dashes named a note length. Its limits were the table (four classes, no
   dotted values, "longer" read back as six units) and a mismatch between the two sides
   (dashes were added after the END of a note but measured from its START, so a two-digit
   fret broke the inverse).
3. August 2025 (`7d132e9`): one dash per smallest note value in the bar, the layout up to
   v0.7.0. The parser stopped inverting the generator at that point.

To me it was clear that gtrsnipe could go back to an older idea; to Claude it was clear that #2 *almost*
was what I'd wanted, and the bugs he found in that implementation told me I got frustrated and abandoned it because I thought
maybe the idea was bad to begin with, not seeing the bugs nor fully implementing the timing table. Now that it is
clear, we are re-implementing the older idea, but doing so correctly.

## The idea in one sentence

**The dashes after a note say how long it lasts**: until the next note, or the bar line.

To the eye it reads the way hand-written tabs do: longer notes get more room, though not in
proportion. To gtrsnipe it is exact, because the *number* of dashes names the length.

## The table

One dash is the *base* note. Each doubling adds two dashes; a dot adds one.

| length, in base notes | 1 | 1½ | 2 | 3 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 12 | 16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| dashes | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |
| with a sixteenth as the base | s | s. | e | e. | q | q. | H | H. | W |

The base is the tune's shortest note unless `--tab-base` names one (see Options). It has to
be at least as short as the shortest note, or two notes would touch and read as one fret
number.

## The rules

1. **A bar is one measure.** It is written `|`, one padding dash, its notes, `|`.
2. **Each onset** is its fret number, then its dashes on every string. The notes of a chord
   start in the same column and share one length: the time to the next onset.
3. **Dashes are counted from the end of the fret number**, so a two-digit fret makes a bar
   wider but never changes what it says.
4. **A rest at the start of a bar** is its dashes after the padding dash: one dash before the
   first note means no rest; more means a rest of that many dashes, less one.
5. **A rest after a note** isn't written. A note's dashes run to the next onset; whether it
   rings or is damped for that time is something a tab doesn't say (`--sustain` decides).
   A note held across a bar line is the same case: its dashes run to the bar line, and the
   next bar opens with a rest.
6. **A bar with no notes** is a silent measure, however wide. gtrsnipe writes `|----|`.
7. **A pickup.** A first bar whose lengths add up to less than a measure is a pickup: its
   notes end at the bar line.
8. **The legend** is a header line. It names the layout and the base, and it is what tells a
   reader to count dashes:

   ```
   // Rhythm: dash-count, base 1/16   (s=1 s.=2 e=3 e.=4 q=5 q.=6 H=7 H.=8 W=9)
   ```

   A tab without a `// Rhythm:` line is read as in v0.7.0: each bar a measure, notes placed
   in proportion to their columns. So older gtrsnipe tabs, tabs from elsewhere, and a tab
   whose comments were left out all still read, approximately.
9. **The check.** A bar's rest and note lengths must add up to a measure (from `// Time:`).
   A bar that doesn't, because someone edited it by hand, is read in proportion like any
   other tab, and the reader can say which bars those were.

## Lengths the table doesn't hold

Five or seven base notes, or triplets mixed with even notes: about 2% of bars on the test
tunes. By default such a bar is written with **columns as time** (see `columns` under
Options), which is exact, and the legend names it:

```
// Rhythm: dash-count, base 1/16; bars 7, 12 in columns
```

`--tab-odd-bars` chooses otherwise: `nearest` writes the nearest lengths the table holds and
names the bars as approximate; `error` stops and lists them, for when every bar must be in
one layout.

## Examples

A sixteenth-note run, then a whole note (base: a sixteenth). The second bar contains just one fret number,
with enough room to say "this is not another sixteenth", and nowhere near in proportion.

```
e|-0-1-3-5-7-5-3-1-0-1-3-5-7-5-3-1-|-0---------|
```

*Ode to Joy*, bars 1 to 4 (base: an eighth, the tune's shortest note; `e=1 q=3 q.=4 H=5`).
Bar 4 is a dotted quarter, an eighth and a half:

```
B|-5---5---6---8---|-8---6---5---3---|-1---1---3---5---|-5----3-3-----|
```

*Battle Hymn of the Republic*, bars 1 and 2 (base: a sixteenth; `s=1 e.=4 H=7`), with one-
and two-digit frets:

```
e|----------------------8----10-|-12----12-12----10-8-------|
B|-8----8-8----6-5----8---------|---------------------------|
```

A quarter rest and three quarter notes; a silent bar; two half-note chords (base: a quarter;
`q=1 H=3`):

```
e|--0-3-8-|----|---------|
B|--------|----|---------|
G|--------|----|-0---0---|
D|--------|----|-2---0---|
A|--------|----|-3---2---|
E|--------|----|-----3---|
```

## Width and rows

- A note costs its digits plus its dashes, so a bar's width depends on how many notes it has
  and not on how long its silences are. With one-digit frets a bar is never wider than two
  columns per base note, plus one: 33 columns for a 4/4 bar in sixteenths, 17 in eighths.
  With two-digit frets, 49 and 25.
- On 300 folk tunes this layout came to 1.3 times the v0.7.0 total width, with 98% of bars
  exactly decodable (v0.7.0: 33%). With the base fixed at a sixteenth it is 1.6 times.
- Rows work as before: whole bars only, as many as fit `--max-line-width`, and a bar wider than
  the limit gets a row to itself. **The default width becomes 80 columns** (it was 40, a
  leftover from reading tabs on a phone).

## What it doesn't do

- **Two voices with different rhythms.** An onset has one length.
- **Other tools.** A reader that takes columns as time will get the order and the bars right
  and the lengths squeezed. For such readers there is `--tab-rhythm columns`, which anyone
  can take at face value. For programs that must have the rhythm exactly, a structured file is
  the better carrier (parking lot: a `.gp` file for the tab editors).

## Options

Every choice here is an option with a default. All three layouts stay; none is a stage on
the way to another.

- **`--tab-rhythm {dashes,columns,loose}`** (default `dashes`).
  - `dashes`: this layout.
  - `columns`: columns as time. Each bar is cut into equal slots, one per its shortest step,
    two columns wide (three in a bar with a two-digit fret), and a note is written at the
    start of its slot. Exact, readable with no legend (`// Rhythm: columns` still says so),
    and sometimes wide: a bar mixing short and long notes can pass 80 columns.
  - `loose`: the layout up to v0.7.0, with no `// Rhythm:` line. Compact, and approximate.
- **`--tab-base {auto,1/4,1/8,1/16,1/32}`** (default `auto`, the tune's shortest note). A
  fixed base means one table for every tab, at the cost of width.
- **`--tab-odd-bars {columns,nearest,error}`** (default `columns`): see above.
- **`--tab-letters`** (off by default): a line of note lengths over the staff, on top of any
  layout. Long notes are capitals and short ones lowercase, like the prefixes of units:
  `W H q e s t`, with a dot for a dotted note. A letter with nothing under it is a rest.
  Read back in either case.
- **`--max-line-width`** (default 80).
- Leaving comment lines out is its own item (parking lot, `--omit-comments`). Without the
  `// Rhythm:` line a tab is still a tab; it just reads approximately.

This changes how every tab looks by default, so it ships as a minor version.

## Prior practice

None of this is new in kind. Howard Wright's 1995 guide to tab notation says spacing shows
"which notes are the long ones, and which are the short and fast ones" but "will not tell you
exactly how long", describes writing letters "above the corresponding note" (q for a quarter,
e for an eighth), and adds that there is "no particular 'standard' way" and that it is
"essential to explain the system you use" (<https://www.classtab.org/tabbing.htm>). The
legend line is that explanation.
